the experimental chapters. Each section corresponds to a specific step in the
analysis and is intended to provide enough detail for reproducibility without
interrupting the flow of the main text. Section~\ref{app:scaling} derives the

practical lessons learned during the laboratory and clinical measurement sessions
that are relevant to future iterations of the protocol.


\section{Sample Calculation for Scaling at 30~G}
\label{app:scaling}

This appendix supports Section~\ref{sec:translationalForceBG} and
Table~\ref{tab:chi_scaling}. The goal is to determine the minimum magnetic
susceptibility a laboratory test object must possess to produce a measurable
deflection under reduced field conditions, and to verify that the same
force-to-weight ratio encountered clinically can be reproduced at bench scale.

From the ASTM~F2052 force balance (Appendix~X3 therein \cite{astmF2052}),
the equilibrium deflection angle satisfies
\begin{equation}
	\tan(\alpha) = \frac{\chi_m}{\rho\,\mu_0\,g}
	\cdot B_0\frac{dB_0}{dz},
	\label{eq:forFit_app}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$\,H/m, $g = 9.807$\,m/s$^2$, $\rho$ is the
material density, and $B_0\,dB_0/dz$ is the magnetic force product at the
test location. The product $\chi_m/\rho$ and the field term appear
symmetrically: to reproduce the same $\tan(\alpha)$ at a lower field, the
ratio $\chi_m/\rho$ must increase in inverse proportion,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\chi_m}{\rho}\bigg|_{\mathrm{lab}}
	= \frac{\mu_0\,g\,\tan\alpha}
	{\left(B_0\,\dfrac{dB_0}{dz}\right)_{\!\mathrm{lab}}}.
	\label{eq:chi_rho_lab}
\end{equation}

The on-axis field of a finite solenoid of radius $R$ and length $L$,
normalized to a central field $B_0$, is
\begin{equation}
	B_z(z) = \frac{B_0}{2\,B_{ctr}}
	\left[
	\frac{z + L/2}{\sqrt{R^2+(z+L/2)^2}}
	-
	\frac{z - L/2}{\sqrt{R^2+(z-L/2)^2}}
	\right],
\end{equation}
where $B_{ctr} = (L/2)\big/\sqrt{R^2+(L/2)^2}$ is the analytic
center value. The force product $B_z\,dB_z/dz$ peaks near the coil exit,
which is the maximum-sensitivity test location prescribed by
ASTM~F2052 \cite{astmF2052}.

A bench solenoid with $R = 5$\,cm and $L = 20$\,cm was modeled at
$B_0 = 30$\,G $= 3\times10^{-3}$\,T. The maximum force product occurs at
$z \approx 8.7$\,cm from the solenoid center (near the coil exit), where
\begin{equation}
	\left(B_0\frac{dB_0}{dz}\right)_{\!\mathrm{lab}}
	= 6.09\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{T^2/m}.
\end{equation}
Assuming a target deflection of $\tan\alpha = 0.01$ (1\,cm over a 1\,m
pendulum string) and substituting into Equation~(\ref{eq:chi_rho_lab}),
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\chi_m}{\rho}
	= \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(9.807)(0.01)}{6.09\times10^{-5}}
	= 2.02\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/kg}.
\end{equation}
For a laboratory test material with $\rho = 7900$\,kg/m$^3$ that of common grades of steel,
\begin{equation}
	\chi_m = 2.02\times10^{-3} \times 7900 \approx 15.98.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Comparison with clinical conditions}

ASTM~F2052-15 reports an upper bound of
$|B_0\,\nabla B_0| < 48$\,T$^2$/m for current 3\,T clinical
systems \cite{astmF2052}. The ratio of the clinical to laboratory force
product is
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\left(B_0\,\nabla B_0\right)_{\!3\,\mathrm{T}}}
	{\left(B_0\,\nabla B_0\right)_{\!\mathrm{lab}}}
	= \frac{48}{6.09\times10^{-5}}
	\approx 7.9\times10^{5},
\end{equation}
and the required $\chi_m/\rho$ scales inversely by the same factor. The
full results across field levels are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:chi_scaling}.

For reference, Woods et al.\ \cite{Woods2021_MRIdisplacement} measured deflection angles for 11 common implant alloys on a Siemens Trio 3\,T system under $|B_0\,\nabla B_0| \approx 3.5$--$4.6$\,T$^2$/m. Back-calculating from their measured force ratios, the highest product of strength and gradient yielded a $\chi_m/\rho$ of $4.2\times10^{-7}$\,m$^3$/kg for 316L stainless steel. This is $\sim\!4800$ times smaller than the laboratory threshold in Equation~(\ref{eq:chi_rho_lab}), confirming that none of the tested clinical alloys would produce a detectable deflection under the assumed bench field conditions.

\section{Smallest measurable force of translational fixture}
\label{app:smallestForce}

Following section \ref{sec:Smallestmeasurable} For a specimen of $m = 0.1\,\text{g} = 1\times10^{-4}\,\text{kg}$, the smallest measurable magnetic force is given by equation \ref{eq:minForce}

\begin{equation}
	F_{m,\min} = m g \frac{z}{L}, \quad	\text{(Eq.~\ref{eq:minForce})} \nonumber 
\end{equation}

\noindent where $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, $z = 1\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}$, and $L = 1\,\text{m}$. Substituting these values yields

\begin{align}
	F_{m,\min} &= (1\times10^{-4})(9.81)\left(\frac{1\times10^{-3}}{1}\right) \nonumber\\
	&= 9.81\times10^{-7}\,\text{N} \approx 1.0\times10^{-6}\,\text{N}.
\end{align}

\noindent Thus, for a $0.1\,\text{g}$ mass, the smallest detectable magnetic force is approximately $1\,\mu\text{N}$. The corresponding force ratio remains 
\begin{equation}
	\frac{F_m}{F_g} = \frac{z}{L} = 10^{-3},
\end{equation}
\noindent indicating that the setup can detect magnetic forces as small as one-thousandth of the object’s weight.

\section{Smallest measurable torque on fixture}
\label{app:smallestTorque}

Following Section~\ref{sec:SmallTorque}, we evaluate the strain, voltage response, and force detection limit using the geometry reported by Chiu, \textit{et al.} (2016) \cite{Chiu2016CharacterizationOA} For the Nitinol wire,

\begin{align*}
	L &= 69.85\,\text{mm} = 6.985\times10^{-2}\,\text{m}, \\
	D &= 0.64\,\text{mm} = 6.604\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}, \\
	E &= 66.2\,\text{GPa} = 6.62\times10^{10}\,\text{Pa}.
\end{align*}

The geometric sensitivity term appearing in Equation~\ref{eq:strain_beam} is

\begin{equation}
	\frac{L}{D^3}.
\end{equation}

Substituting numerical values,

\begin{align}
	D^3 &= (6.604\times10^{-4})^3 
	= 2.88\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}^3, \nonumber\\
	\frac{L}{D^3} &= \frac{6.985\times10^{-2}}{2.88\times10^{-10}} 
	= 2.43\times10^{8}\,\text{m}^{-2}.
\end{align}

\noindent This agrees with the order-of-magnitude sensitivity reported in Section~\ref{sec:SmallTorque}.

Using Equation~\ref{eq:strain_beam}, the surface strain produced by the minimum detectable force 
$F = 0.49\,\text{mN} = 4.9\times10^{-4}\,\text{N}$, as reported by Chiu, \textit{et al.} (2016) \cite{Chiu2016CharacterizationOA} is

\begin{equation}
	\epsilon = \frac{32 F L}{\pi E D^3}.
\end{equation}

Substituting,

\begin{align}
	\epsilon &= 
	\frac{32 (4.9\times10^{-4})(6.985\times10^{-2})}
	{\pi (6.62\times10^{10})(2.88\times10^{-10})} \nonumber\\
	&= 1.82\times10^{-5}.
\end{align}

\noindent Thus, the minimum detectable force corresponds to a strain of approximately
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon \approx 20\,\mu\varepsilon.
\end{equation}

Assuming a half-bridge configuration (Equation~\ref{eq:bridge_output}) with
\begin{align*}
	G_F &= 2, \\
	V_{exc} &= 5\,\text{V},
\end{align*}
the predicted output voltage is

\begin{align}
	V_{out} &\approx \frac{V_{exc}}{2} G_F \epsilon \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{5}{2}(2)(2.0\times10^{-5}) \nonumber\\
	&= 5(2.0\times10^{-5}) \nonumber\\
	&= 1.0\times10^{-4}\,\text{V}.
\end{align}

\noindent Therefore,
\begin{equation}
	V_{out} \approx 0.1\,\text{mV}.
\end{equation}

Additional amplification is therefore required to reach the experimentally observed 
$\sim 3\,\text{mV}$ signal level.

However, using Chiu, \textit{et al.} (2016) reported gain and assuming noise at

\begin{align*}
	V_{noise}  &= 3 mV, \\
	G &= 100,
\end{align*}

We got an $\epsilon_{min}$ of 

\begin{align}
	\epsilon_{min} &= 
			\frac{2 V_{noise}}{V_{exc} G_F \quad \text{gain}} \\
	&=	\frac{2 (0.003)}{5 (2) (100)} \\
	&=	6 \mu\varepsilon.
\end{align}

Using Equation~\ref{eq:force_limit}, the minimum detectable force for a given minimum strain $\epsilon_{min}$ is

\begin{equation}
	F_{min} = \frac{\pi E D^3}{32 L}\,\epsilon_{min}.
\end{equation}

If the system resolves strains on the order of $1\,\mu\varepsilon = 6\times10^{-6}$, then

\begin{align}
	F_{min} &= 
	\frac{\pi (6.62\times10^{10})(2.88\times10^{-10})}
	{32(6.985\times10^{-2})}
	(6\times10^{-6}) \nonumber\\
	&= 0.161 \text{mN}.
\end{align}

\noindent This confirms micro-Newton sensitivity becomes achievable with reduced noise, increased $L/D^3$, 
and signal averaging.

\section{Kirchhoff analysis of Circuit}
\label{app:Kirchhoff}
\begin{figure}[h!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{assets/opAmp.png}
	\caption[Schematic of the Wheatstone bridge circuit used for torque sensing]{Schematic of the Wheatstone bridge with strain gauges $S1$ and $S2$, fixed resistors $R=R1=R2$, and operational amplifier with feedback resistor $R_f$. This configuration corresponds to the transfer function in Equation~\eqref{eq:kirchov12}.}
	\label{fig:opamp_bridge}
\end{figure}


Using \gls{kcl}, in Figure \ref{fig:opamp_bridge} for node $V_A$ which corresponds to the positive leg of the op amp, or non inverting voltage, we have

\begin{align*}
	\frac{V_A - V_{in}}{S1} + \frac{V_A}{S2} &= 0 \\
	\frac{S2 (V_A - V_{in}) + S1 V_A}{\cancel{S1 S2}} &= 0 \\
	V_A (S2 + S1) - S2 V_{in} &= 0 \\
	V_A &= \frac{S2 V_{in}}{S2 + S1}.
\end{align*}

Similarly, for the node $V_B$ corresponding to the inverting input of the op-amp

\begin{align*}
	\frac{V_B - V_{in}}{R} + \frac{V_V}{R} + \frac{V_B - V_{out}}{R_f} &= 0 \\
	\frac{R_f (2V_B - V_{in}) + R (V_B - V{out})}{\cancel{R R_f}} &= 0 \\
	V_B (2R_f + R) - R_f V_{in} - RV_{out} &= 0 \\
	V_B &= \frac{R_f V_{in} + R V_{out}}{2R_f + R}.
\end{align*}

At this point, we make a standard ideal op-amp assumption, the op-amp has infinite open-loop gain, meaning any finite output voltage corresponds to a vanishingly small input voltage difference. That is, for the difference amplifier

\begin{equation}
    V_{out} = A_{\text{ol}} (V_A - V_B), \quad A_{\text{ol}} \rightarrow \infty,
\end{equation}

which implies $V_A \approx V_B$. Substituting $V_A = V_B$ into the previous equations allows us to solve for the closed-loop transfer function

\begin{align*}
	\frac{S2 V_{in}}{S2+S1}  &= \frac{R_f V_{in} + R V_{out}}{2R_f + R} \\
 \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}&= 	\frac{R_f (S2 - S1) + S2 R}{R (S2 + S1)} \\
\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}&= 		\frac{R_f}{R} \frac{S2 - S1}{S2 + S1} + \frac{S2}{S2 + S1} \tag{\ref{eq:kirchov12}}
\end{align*}

\noindent
In summary, the output voltage is composed of two contributions: a differential term amplified by the ratio $R_f / R = 160$, which captures the imbalance between the strain gauges $S1$ and $S2$, and a baseline voltage division term $S2 / (S2 + S1)$ that represents the inherent bridge offset. This expression clearly shows how the Wheatstone bridge imbalance is amplified by the op-amp to produce the measurable output.


\subsection{Explaining the behavior of Coarse-Fine Tuning with trim resistors in Circuit}
\label{app:pots}

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.11\textwidth]{Assets/pots.png}
	\caption[Schematic of the coarse–fine trim resistors configuration]{Schematic of the coarse–fine trim resistors (POTs) configuration used for the voltage-divider derivations in Figure \ref{fig:torqueCircuit}.}
	\label{fig:pots}
\end{figure}

Starting with KVL around the loop

\begin{equation}
V_{\text{in}} - I R_{1} - I R_{2} = 0,
\end{equation}
Factor out the current
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{in}} = I (R_{1}+R_{2}),
\end{equation}
Solve for the series current
\begin{equation}
I = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_{1}+R_{2}},
\end{equation}
The node voltage is the drop across the lower resistor \(R_{2}\)
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{node}} = I\,R_{2} = 
\left( \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_{1}+R_{2}} \right) R_{2}.
\end{equation}
Final voltage-divider form
\begin{equation}
\boxed{V_{\text{node}} = V_{\text{in}}\,
	\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}+R_{2}} }. \tag{\ref{eq:voltageDivider}}
\end{equation}

For these evaluations
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{in}} = 5.0~\text{V}, \qquad
R_{1}=10~\text{k}\Omega, \qquad
R_{2} \in [0,1000]~\Omega.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Halfway Fine Adjustment \(\,R_{2}=500~\Omega\)}
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{node}} 
= 5.0\,
\frac{500}{10000 + 500}
= 5.0 \left( \frac{500}{10500} \right)
= 5.0 (0.047619)
= 0.2381~\text{V}.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
V_{\text{node}} = 5\,
\frac{R_{2}}{10000 + R_{2}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
		\hline
		Fine Pot Setting & $R_{2}$ (Ohms) & $V_{\text{node}}$ (V) \\
		\hline
		Max & $1000$ & $0.4545$ \\
		Mid & $500$  & $0.2381$ \\
		Min & $0$    & $0$ \\
		\hline
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Now fix the fine pot at
\begin{equation}
R_{2}=500~\Omega.
\end{equation}
The 10 k coarse pot wiper divides $R_{1}$ into two portions.  
Let the coarse wiper position be $\alpha\in[0,1]$, where

\begin{equation}
\alpha = 0 : \text{wiper at top}, \quad
\alpha = 1 : \text{wiper at bottom}.
\end{equation}

The wiper voltage is a weighted average between the supply (5 V)  
and the node voltage computed with the fixed fine setting
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{node,fine}=500}
= 0.2381~\text{V}.
\end{equation}
Thus the coarse-wiper voltage is
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{wiper}} = (1-\alpha)(5.0) + \alpha (0.2381).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Coarse-Wiper Table (Fine Fixed at Half)}
\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
		\hline
		Coarse Setting & $\alpha$ & $V_{\text{wiper}}$ (V) \\
		\hline
		Top (Min Coarse)    & $0$   & $5.000$ \\
		Midpoint            & $0.5$ & $2.619$ \\
		Bottom (Max Coarse) & $1$   & $0.238$ \\
		\hline
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $\alpha=0$ (coarse at minimum), the wiper sits at the full input voltage
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{wiper}} = 5~\text{V},
\end{equation}
which is independent of the fine adjustment.  
At $\alpha=1$ (coarse at maximum), the wiper voltage collapses into the range controlled solely by the fine pot
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{wiper}} = V_{\text{node,fine}}.
\end{equation}
Thus the coarse pot sets the overall span,  
and the fine pot determines the precise lower-region tuning.

\subsection{Circuit design caveat}
\label{app:circuit_caveat}

From the torque methodology developed in Section \ref{sec:InLab_Torque}, a set of neodymium magnets was introduced to increase the magnetic field strength to 300 G, compared to the 30 G provided by the Helmholtz coil, because the short rod in the smaller fields produced signals that were too small to yield reliable results. The calibration established a detection limit of $\tau_{\min} = 0.012,\text{mN}\cdot\text{m}$. For the short rod in the Helmholtz configuration the maximum field strength was $B_{\max} = 3.85,\text{mT}$), and using the $\chi_m$ obtained from the neodymium-based fit, the expected torque was $\tau_{\text{expected}} = 0.26,\text{mN}\cdot\text{m}$. This corresponds to a ratio of $\tau_{\text{expected}} / \tau_{\min} \approx 21.7$, which is well above the detection threshold in principle.

A likely explanation is the influence of noise in the measurement circuit, particularly from the trim resistors when the signal approaches the detection limit. During calibration, a noise level of approximately 0.8 mV was observed when the trim resistors were used to null the signal prior to applying load. However, this was variable across sensors, as noted by Dr. Rutel (University of Oklahoma), placing the balancing trim resistor at the output of the amplifier, Figure \ref{fig:pots} right after Figure \ref{fig:opamp_bridge}, effectively suppresses part of the measurable signal. Incorporating the trim resistors directly into the Wheatstone bridge arm would instead allow null balancing at the bridge level, eliminating residual offset at the source and improving sensitivity for low-torque measurements.


\section{Methodology notes}
\label{app:MethodologyNotes}

The following paragraphs describe the methods troubleshooting and practical improvement on the methodology. For a complete description of the final version of the methodologies applied on this work see \ref{ch:matsNmets}

For the translational force experiment in the lab (Section \ref{sec:experiment1}), initial trials attempted to map the magnetic field from the furthest position of the pendulum, 8 cm from the solenoid face, at a constant solenoid current in a single run. The idea was to obtain ($B_0$) at the pendulum’s resting position and then compute the field gradient at any point from that map. Afterward, the pendulum was allowed to be slowly pulled toward the solenoid to its equilibrium position so its displacement (in cm) could be recorded. This was repeated while increasing the solenoid current in 0.5 A steps, aiming to fit the resulting equilibrium positions using Eq. \ref{eq:forFit}. 

These attempts produced inconsistent results that pointed to errors inherent to the setup. Because the current, rather than the entire apparatus position, was changed, the value of $B_0$ at the pendulum’s resting position did not correspond to the required by Eq. \ref{eq:forFit}. Even using the mapped field, estimating the correct value for the magnetic field for each equilibrium position was unreliable. Moreover, predicting equilibrium positions before running the experiment proved difficult. Additional complications came from solenoid imperfections, current leakage, and induced currents, all of which became significant once displacements exceeded 1 cm. At higher fields the pendulum was sometimes pulled fully into the solenoid. 

Another factor was the gaussmeter restriction on accurate motion tracking to roughly 11–12 mm on the translation stage. While this approach resembles the proposed clinical protocol, the scale of the apparatus and the nonuniform fields make it impractical. Once the approach was inverted, as explained in Section \ref{sec:experiment1} the methodology, deflection angle measurments resulted in $\chi_m$ values with 0.5\% precision, validating the methodology.

In the clinic, a single mapping session gave both B and $\nabla$B at any point, and left the angle measurement as the critical step (see Section \ref{sec:clinicTrans}). However, the initial attempt revealed practical challenges with string attachment to the baskets. Knotting proved time-consuming and inconsistent across different basket designs, leading to variability in suspension length and tension. To streamline future sessions, pre-prepared assemblies are recommended: additional 3D-printed baskets should be fabricated, each paired with a fixed-length string segment of approximately 1.3\,m, with \glspl[]{mut} attached pre-weighted, and labeled for rapid deployment. Similarly on the torque pipeline (see Section \ref{sec:clinicTorque}), multiple lever arms of identical size should be fabricated in advance, each with \gls{mut} samples pre-attached to ensure consistent mounting and to facilitate rapid swapping during trials.

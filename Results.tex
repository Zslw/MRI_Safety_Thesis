Usually epicardial leads are labeled \gls{mri}-conditional at best \cite{schaller2021,fda2023testing}. Leaving uncertainties on whether patients with these leads can undergo a \gls{mri} scan, despite the fact that these patient population is more likely to need one. 

The different lead types, uni-polar vs bipolar, materials they are made of, size and configuration, and case by case complications pose a real challenge in the identification and testing of these leads under a \gls{mri} environment. Moreover, the effects on the leads are split by physical interaction. Being three the major concerns RF induced-heating, magnetically induced forces and torques, and electrical currents produced by gradient encoders.

In Chapter \ref{ch:background} an explanation on the physics for the particular case of magnetically induced force and torques is given. Leaning into our methodology which has a three step approach with expected measurement limits. 

For this chapter, we present the results found on each of the experiments parallel to Chapter \ref{ch:matsNmets}, that is Magnetic field mapping, Translational Force Measurements, and Torque measurements. In each of these experiments, the focus is on both laboratory work and clinical work. First, the laboratory work shows the expected behavior in a safe, controllable manner and allows us to predict the results on the clinic. Second, the clinical work will orient us and allow for assumption check and methodology fixes. This changes will be addressed in Chapter \ref{ch:disCon}.

\section{Key Findings}

All measured translational force ratios remained below the ASTM F2052-15 threshold of 1.0. The custom fixtures successfully resolved micro-Newton-scale effects on lightweight epicardial leads. However, the Medtronic STREAMLINE 6942 lead showed greater pendulum deflection than expected under translational testing, and torque measurem	ents on the small reference cylinder were limited by sensor sensitivity in weak Helmholtz-coil fields. In the clinical 3T environment, full quantitative torque acquisition could not be completed.

The results can be summarized in sequence as follows. First, magnetic field mapping confirmed the expected decay, with $|B\nabla B|\approx ranging from 13-23$~T$^2$/m at the bore entrance in both laboratory and clinical settings. Next, translational force measurements demonstrated that the epicardial lead produced force ratios $\text{FR}=\tan\alpha < 1.0$, with a maximum deflection of $\alpha\approx 5.3^\circ$. Finally, torque calibration curves were linear, laboratory-scale measurements succeeded, although clinical torque runs were incomplete due to time constraints.

The successful collaboration between Creighton University and CHI Health CUMC-Bergan Mercy enabled in-clinic data acquisition on a working 3T Siemens Magnetom\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} scanner. All custom fixtures proved MRI-safe, non-interfering, and functional in the clinical environment. Acquiring real-world translational force and field-mapping data constitutes a significant achievement in itself and validates the entire experimental framework developed in this thesis.

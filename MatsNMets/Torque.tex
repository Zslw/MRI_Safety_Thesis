The final part of this force and torque assessment involves the measurement of the magnetically induced torque on a horizontally oriented object. ASTM 2213-17 \cite{astm2213} lists five different methodologies, calculation based on measured displacement force method, low friction surface method, suspension method, torsional spring method, and pulley method. In section \ref{sec:LitRev}, the results on three of these methods were revised. Those included low friction, pulley and a variation of torsional spring methods \cite{Woods2021_MRIdisplacement,stoianovici2024,Seo2021767}, which showed good results but further considerations are needed. 

Similar to suspension method or string method, we developed a torque measurement device capable of holding both a custom made sensor and test object. The next section will explain further specific physics and the fixture's  components, which include sensor and circuit implementation explained in subsection \ref{sec:circuitDesignNsensor}.

\subsection{In Lab Methodology and Setup}
\label{sec:InLab_Torque}
With transfer arm length (5cm) and sensor position (tip at rulers farther edge) optimized and confirmed, the method for the experiment goes as follows: a increasingly stronger magnetic flied is applied to the test object initially positioned such that it makes a approx 45$^\circ$ angle with the magnetic field. The magnetic field is monitored on each increment and recorded in Gauss using the AlphaLab Inc. Gaussmeter, while the sensor and circuit are paired with a \gls{daq} and computer for voltage measurements.

To assess different torque strengths, two test objects of the same material and different lengths were considered. First a long steel solid cylinder of about 20 cm length by  diameter, that way a strong dipole moment could be induced to it, serving as a visual confirmation that the system is working. Second, a smaller cylinder of about 2cm long and same diameter served as the main test object as we aim to measure the smallest magnetically induced torque possible. Following the same logic as in translational experiment this torque scales proportionally following equation \ref{eq:torqueCal}.
 
Figures \ref{fig:equipment_list_zoomed} and \ref{fig:torque_compare} show the experimental array. To ensure a wide range of magnetic field strengths capable to interact with both test cylinders we arrange two different sources, a Helmholtz coil and a set of neodymium magnets.

A second setup (Figure \ref{fig:helmholtzsetup}) utilized Helmholtz Coils EM‑6722 from PASCO Scientific to create a controlled, nearly uniform static magnetic field that could be varied in strength and observed in real time. These Helmholtz coils consist of two identical coils, each wound with 200 turns, they were wired so current flowed in the same direction. They had an inner radius of about 10.06 cm and outer radius around 10.58 cm, and they sit on a optical base that lets us adjust the center-to-center spacing close to where the field is most uniform (about one diameter apart). The coils used a maximum DC current of 2 A, which we supply with the PASCO Scientific SF-9584 DC power supply to increase the magnetic field incrementally; as current increases, so too does the magnetic flux density between the coils, allowing us to observe corresponding torque changes on our contraption.

\subsection{Torque Measurement Apparatus}
\label{sec:TorqMeasureApp}

For the torque estimation, ASTM 2213-17 \cite{astm2213} lists five different methodologies. Calculation based on measured displacement force method, low friction surface method, suspension method, torsional spring method, and pulley method. In section \ref{sec:LitRev}, the results on three of these methods were revised. Those included low friction, pulley and a variation of torsional spring methods \cite{Woods2021_MRIdisplacement,stoianovici2024,Seo2021767}, which showed good results but further considerations are needed. In our case a torque measurement device, previously shown in Figure \ref{fig:parameters}, capable of holding both the sensor and subject was developed and will be further explained in section \ref{sec:TorqMeasureApp}. In addition to the sensor and circuit developed in section \ref{sec:circuitDesignNsensor}, a \gls{daq} system and a computer with a \gls{lvi} was needed. 


On the final part of the assessment, the magnetically induced torque on a horizontally oriented subject was measured. To achieve this, a non-magnetic pole was fabricated with clinical considerations in mind. Figure \ref{fig:parameters} shows the pole, which was made from Delrin\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} and is 200 mm tall with 51 mm of diameter it can be attached to a PVC base for support with a nylon screw and it features a space for a non-magnetic bearing to sit. This non-magnetic ceramic bearing is meant to reduce as much friction for the rotation as possible while keeping everything non magnetic. Figure \ref{fig:bearing} shows a close up to the bearing, which has a plastic notch capable of holding a 10 mm thick ruler (lever arm) to spin. 

The induced torque on the subject, explained in section \ref{sec:torqueBG}, is transmitted mechanically to a non-magnetic ruler which then would apply a force on the sensor introduced in section \ref{sec:circuitDesignNsensor}. This sensor would bend on and the counter-force would be measured using strain gauges.

\subsubsection{Magnetic field conditions}

For the initial configuration, a permanent-magnet–based torque setup was implemented as shown in Figure \ref{fig:neodymiumset}. This first arrangement employed four neodymium magnets, each magnetized through their thickness, with nominal dimensions of 1/4 in thickness and 5/16 in outer diameter. The magnets were mounted symmetrically on a rigid mechanical frame, with their magnetization axes oriented to produce a static magnetic field across the region occupied by the torque arm. Threaded lateral adjusters and a central support allow controlled positioning and alignment of the magnets relative to the rotating element.

A second setup (Figure \ref{fig:helmholtzsetup}) utilized Helmholtz Coils EM‑6722 from PASCO Scientific to create a controlled, nearly uniform static magnetic field that could be varied in strength and observed in real time. These Helmholtz coils consist of two identical coils, each wound with 200 turns, they were wired so current flowed in the same direction. They had an inner radius of about 10.06 cm and outer radius around 10.58 cm, and they sit on a optical base that lets us adjust the center-to-center spacing close to where the field is most uniform (about one diameter apart). The coils used a maximum DC current of 2 A, which we supply with the PASCO Scientific SF-9584 DC power supply to increase the magnetic field incrementally; as current increases, so too does the magnetic flux density between the coils, allowing us to observe corresponding torque changes on our contraption.



\subsubsection{In Clinic Measurement Protocol}

Torque measurements were conducted ensuring consistency and reproducibility. Prior to setup, both researchers confirmed that all personal belongings and ferromagnetic items had been removed from their pockets. The \gls{lvi} was opened with the necessary parameters configured, and the \gls{dac} was powered on.  A 10-meter jumper wire was connected to the \gls{dac} and integrated into the circuit, with the computer system interfaced to the \gls{dac} for real-time data collection. 

The experimental apparatus was then fitted with the test object—either the magnetically susceptible calibration material or the epicardial lead—mounted on the rotating ruler. Then it was positioned on the MRI room bench at a distance of 1 meter from the bore, measured from the base of the apparatus to the bore face. Finally, the ruler arm was aligned to rest against the strain gauge, oriented as close as possible to 45° relative to the bore axis. 

In the control room, torque measurements were acquired according to a predefined flowchart procedure, with results recorded on the measurement worksheet. A section of the worksheet was considered complete only when all data fields were marked with an “X.” Section 1 of the worksheet corresponded to measurements performed with the magnetically susceptible calibration object. Following these measurements, the apparatus was retrieved from the MRI room, and the calibration object was replaced with the epicardial lead affixed to the ruler. Section 2 of the worksheet was then completed by repeating the flowchart process with the lead in place.

Data collection in each section was performed at incremental positions along the MRI bore axis. For both the calibration object (Section 1) and the epicardial lead (Section 2), torque measurements were acquired at eleven translational positions: 100, 90, 80, 70, 60, 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, and 0 cm from the bore face. This systematic sampling allowed for direct comparison of torque response between the calibration object and the epicardial lead across the same magnetic field gradient.

To assess \gls{mri}-induced translational forces, our lab developed a pendulum-style apparatus or fixture following the geometry and physics outlined in ASTM F2052-15 \cite{astmF2052}. This apparatus enables measurement of small deflection angles to derive displacement forces via gravitational torque balance (see Eq.~\ref{eq:deflectionAngle}). The following subsections describe the fixture design and features, the test objects used (including positive controls and epicardial lead holders), the smallest measurable force and associated limitations, and the measurement protocols implemented both in the laboratory (low-field solenoid) and in the clinical 3T MRI environment.

\subsection{Measurement Device and test objects}
\label{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}

Figure \ref{fig:pendulum_full} presents CAD renders of the pendulum fixture (full assembly and detailed views of the upper and lower rings), created in CATIA to document the design intent and engineering drawings (full set in Appendix \ref{app:A1}). The fixture was constructed entirely from \gls{pvc} components, including a vertical post of about 1.5 meters and a rigid base of dimensions 30(L)$\times$20(W)$\times$4.5(H) cm for stabilization. This distances were measured from the last contact point of the string to the rubber on the top ring to the sample hanging free at ruler's height. The lower ring, in Figure \ref{fig:realpendulum_lower}, features a slit where a custom made Kydex\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} ruler is held by smaller nylon screws. \subsection{In Lab Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:experiment1}

The solenoid magnet was particularly useful for generating a variable magnetic field for the translational forces measurement tests. It allows for a range of magnetic fields strengths ($B_0$) at a given distance from the magnet without the need of displacing our fixture but essentially needs a different approach to characterizing it. 

\subsubsection*{Alternate Magnetic Field Mapping for This Experiment}

The magnetic field characterization used here differs from Section \ref{sec:labMagnets}. In that earlier mapping, the Gaussmeter probe was translated along the solenoid axis at a fixed current to produce a spatial field profile. For the translational force experiment, this approach was inverted. The pendulum was held stationary at a fixed position (7--8\,cm from the solenoid face), and the solenoid current was varied to increase through a range of magnetic field strengths $B_0$. At each current level, the field was sampled at the pendulum's equilibrium point and at $\pm$0.5\,mm around it, yielding $B_0$, $B_{-0.5}$, and $B_{+0.5}$ for gradient computation.

This approach was chosen because it decouples the mechanical equilibrium of the pendulum from the field scanning motion, ensuring the fixture remains stationary while the magnetic force is varied. As a consequence, the z-axis of the resulting field maps represents the pendulum's equilibrium displacement from its gravitational rest position, not a physical scan of the magnet axis at fixed current. Each data point therefore corresponds to a different applied current at the new equilibrium point rather than a mere change probe location.

Figure~\ref{fig:EquilibriumPointsField} illustrates the three quantities derived from this mapping procedure. The central field $B_0$, the axial gradient $dB/dz$, and their product $B_0\,dB/dz$, which together form the field-side term of Equation~\ref{eq:forFit}. This figure documents the operating range of the solenoid used in the experiment and confirms that $B_0\,dB/dz$ varies monotonically with equilibrium position, a necessary condition for the linear fit described in Section~\ref{sec:res_labscale-Translational}.

\subsubsection*{Measurement Protocol}

The gaussmeter in the \gls{lvi} was operated in a single mode, with rest periods between trials due to the time required to stabilize the pendulum after each adjustment. This methodology is detailed below.

The setup used our custom-made solenoid connected to a Pasco power supply capable to deliver up 8 A acting as variable magnetic field generator for our experiment. The test object, as described in section \ref{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}, was attached to a string pending from the \gls{mri} safe pole and angle of deflection was measured visually with a ruler on the subject horizontal displacement towards the coil. The gaussmeter was positioned to make magnetic field measurements at the same time as angle deflection.

The translation speed was set up using Kinesis for 0.3 m/s and the maximum displacement of each actuators was 12 mm. The observed displacements for the test object were of about 1 mm/A from the resting position up to 11 mm. Induced currents remained low because the ferromagnetic body barely moved, the translation stage’s full range was used, the object never entered the solenoid unintentionally, and its’s position was always known. For each current applied, the magnetic field was measured at the equilibrium point and recorded alongside the field flux. A magnetic-field map was then acquired at each corresponding current level, ensuring the equilibrium position and its $\pm$0.5 mm neighbors were carefully sampled. With this method, the value of $B_0$ at equilibrium was known for every current level. The gradient was calculated for each point with the two neighbors. Appendix \ref{sec:appCodeNData} includes sample data from these trials.

The measurements taken for characterizing the local field gradient, at the pendulum's equilibrium position ($B_0$), 0.5 mm below ($B_{-0.5}$), and 0.5 mm above ($B_{+0.5}$) were sampled at least nine times per current level, and gradients were computed using Python pandas library with the central finite difference method (Eq. \ref{eq:finiteDiff}) producing

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:finiteDiff1}
	\frac{dB}{dz} \approx \frac{B_{+0.5} - B_{-0.5}}{1\ \text{mm}},
\end{equation}

Using a 1-mm separation ($z_2 - z_1$) keeps the approximation in the linear regime while remaining resolvable on the field map. The 1mm increments in displacement was translated to angular displacement as

\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)
\end{equation}

The translational fixture and its central basket were aligned to scanner isocenter using the room lasers, with the zero position permanently marked using tape on the couch surface. The distance from the couch edge to the fixture base was measured to enable reproducible placement of the equilibrium position. Due to the strong magnetic forces near the bore entrance, achieving no visible displacement of the empty basket was only possible either outside the MR room or with the basket only, positioned far from the 5-gauss line. This meant repeated repositioning of the fixture and multiple attempts to establish a true zero reference, adding considerable time to the protocol.

Measurements were performed using a titanium (Ti) sample as a positive control, given its known paramagnetic response in clinical \gls{mri} contexts. Three replicate trials were conducted with the Ti sample, recording both the distance from isocenter and the observed displacement on the fixture scale at each position. The couch was advanced in 50\,mm increments toward the bore entrance and 100\,mm increments away from it to capture the spatial dependence of the force.

The titanium sample was configured as a loosely coiled spring-like shape, oriented horizontally so that its long axis aligned with the scanner's z-direction (longitudinal field) and its diameter was perpendicular to $B_0$. In contrast, a Medtronic STREAMLINE\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} 6492 unipolar epicardial lead was coiled tightly and oriented vertically (perpendicular to $B_0$), a configuration dictated by its greater thickness and stiffness, which facilitated tighter packing.

Recognizing that coil geometry and orientation strongly influence induced force and torque, additional trials were performed using straight segments of both the titanium material and 316L stainless steel. These straight configurations followed the identical incremental positioning protocol. 

In total, eight raw data tables were acquired, three for the coiled titanium sample, three for the coiled epicardial lead, one for the straight titanium segment, and one for the straight 316L stainless steel segment. All displacement measurements were documented manually and later digitized for analysis. Also publicly available \cite{juarezluna2025mrisafety}.

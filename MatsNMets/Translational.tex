To assess \gls{mri}-induced translational forces, our lab developed a pendulum-style apparatus or fixture following the geometry and physics outlined in ASTM F2052-15 \cite{astmF2052}. This apparatus enables measurement of small deflection angles to derive magnetic forces via gravitational force balance. The following subsections describe the fixture design and features, the test objects used (including positive controls and epicardial lead holders), the smallest measurable force, and the measurement protocols used in both the laboratory (low-field solenoid) and in the clinical 3T MRI environment.

\subsection{Measurement Device and test objects}
\label{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}

Figure \ref{fig:realPendulum} shows the experimental apparatus. Figure \ref{fig:pendulum_full} presents CAD renders of the pendulum fixture created in CATIA to document the design intent and associated engineering drawings (full set in Appendix \ref{app:A1}). The fixture was constructed entirely from \gls{pvc} components, including a vertical post of about 1.5 meters and a rigid base of dimensions 30(L)$\times$20(W)$\times$4.5(H) cm for stabilization, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:realpendulum_full}. The vertical post includes a set of two rings fixed with nylon screws, for height adjustment. A default suspension height of $L= 1$ meter between these two rings was used for consistency across trials, maintaining the geometry and proportions shown in equation (\ref{eq:minForce}). These distances were measured from the last contact point of the string at the rubber interface on the top ring (see Figure \ref{fig:realpendulum_upper}) to the freely hanging sample aligned with the ruler scale. The lower ring, shown in Figure \ref{fig:realpendulum_lower}, features a slit where a custom made Kydex\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} ruler is secured with smaller nylon screws. This ruler is detachable, allowing for easier transportation and interchangeable ruler sizes.

After describing the physical implementation of the pendulum fixture, we now evaluate its measurement capabilities. In particular, we determine the smallest detectable translational  force along the z-axis by analyzing the geometry of the deflected pendulum at equilibrium. Whether forces on the order of millinewtons, comparable to the weight of ~1 g, can be resolved depends on the finite precision of the ruler-based deflection measurement and the pendulum's length.

\subsubsection{Test Objects and Baskets}

%in the lab
Testing for low field lab measurements a solid ferromagnetic cylinder was used as sample, showed in Figure \ref{fig:ferrobject}. Its dimensions were captured with caliper, ruler, and scale, yielding length = 6 mm, diameter = 3 mm, and mass = 0.3648 g therefore density $\rho$= 8.6 g/cm$^3$. The sample was suspended by a 955 mm-long sewing thread, tied with a simple knot at its center. For clinical applications, a 0.25 mm nylon monofilament is recommended. A monofilament nylon thread has low stretch,  complies with MR-safe properties (Non-metallic, Non-conductive, Non-magnetic), and reduces variability introduced by torsional drift or thread elasticity \cite{stoianovici2024, astmF2052}. The bottom of the sample was free to swing, forming a pendulum under gravity effectively aligning with model on Figure \ref{fig:astm_diagram} and Equation \ref{eq:translationalForce}. A horizontal ruler, fixed along the Z-axis, served as the visual reference to quantify lateral displacements.

%positve controls
However, this ferromagnetic test object produces a strong response in the small solenoid field and serves mainly to demonstrate the sensitivity of the pendulum setup in the laboratory. To validate the measurement protocol with materials that are expected to exhibit negligible or zero translational force under clinical \gls{mri} conditions and to characterize the epicardial lead itself, positive controls were prepared from two common biocompatible, non-ferromagnetic metals, titanium and 316L stainless steel wire, both sourced from TEMCo Industrial (USA) and supplied on their standard spools \cite{temco_ti_wire}. 

The straight titanium wire (Surgical Grade 1) had a diameter of 1.02\,mm, a cut length of 2.3\,cm, and a measured mass of 0.1549\,g with handling tape for adhesion. The straight 316L stainless steel wire (annealed medical/industrial grade) had a diameter of 0.76\,mm, a cut length of 2.2\,cm, and a measured mass of 0.1690\,g (including handling tape). An additional titanium coil (mass = 1.7964\,g) was tested as a further positive control to assess geometry effects. 

%in the clinic
The clinical epicardial lead for the clinical trial weighted 138.6\,mg, with a 0.46\,mm diameter and length of 378\,mm. This lead was mounted in a custom 3D-printed \gls{pla} basket, added to easily change test objects while maintaining \gls{mri} compatibility. The lightweight, non-magnetic hexagonal mesh was fabricated from \gls{pla}. The mesh dimensions were 16 (L) $\times$ 14 (W) $\times$ 1 (H) mm, corresponding to a bounding volume of 224 mm$^3$. Due to the hexagonal lattice structure, the effective solid volume is 95.2 mm$^3$, resulting in a mass of at least 118 mg based on PLA material properties. Figure \ref{fig:leadHolder} shows a 3D mesh render with the intended proportions. The corresponding engineering design is provided in Appendix \ref{app:A2}, with STL files available in this Github repository \cite{juarezluna2025mrisafety}. This mesh was 3D printed and has a final weight of 130mg. The mesh was suspended such that the lead axis was aligned approximately parallel to the magnetic field gradient. The mesh's lightweight design aimed to minimize its contribution to the total gravitational force.

\subsection{In Lab Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:experiment1}

The setup used the custom-made solenoid from Section \ref{sec:labMagnets} and the fixture described in Section \ref{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}. Figure \ref{fig:labsetup} shows this experimental laboratory setup used to assess the methodology accuracy and reliability with all components. The test object, shown in Figure \ref{fig:ferrobject}, was attached to a string suspended from the \gls{mri} safe fixture and the angle of deflection was measured visually with a ruler as the object displaced horizontally towards the coil. The gaussmeter probe was positioned to make magnetic field strength and angle deflection measurements at the same time.

The magnetic field characterization used here differs from Section \ref{sec:labMagnets}. In that earlier mapping, the Gaussmeter probe was translated along the solenoid axis, while the current in the solenoid was held. For the translational force experiment, this approach was inverted. The pendulum was held stationary at a fixed position  of 8\,cm from the solenoid face, and the solenoid current was varied to increase through a range of magnetic field strengths $B_0$. This approach was chosen because it decouples the mechanical equilibrium of the pendulum from the field scanning motion, ensuring the fixture remains stationary while the magnetic force is varied. As a consequence, the z-coordinate of the resulting field maps represents the pendulum's displacement from its gravitational rest position (B = 0). Each data point therefore corresponds to a different applied current and field strength at the new equilibrium point. For example, the test object remains at rest (Figure \ref{fig:translational_off}) once the current is flowing the magnetic field on the solenoid pulls the subject towards it (Figure \ref{fig:translational_on}) the more current was applied to the solenoid the stronger the magnetic field and stronger the force.

Induced currents remained low because the ferromagnetic body barely moved, the translation stage’s full range was used, the object never entered the solenoid unintentionally, and its’s position was always known. For each current applied, the magnetic field was measured at the equilibrium point and recorded alongside the field flux. A magnetic-field map was acquired at each corresponding current level, ensuring the equilibrium position and its $\pm$0.5 mm neighbors were sampled at least nine times. With this method, the value of $B_0$ at equilibrium was known for every current level. The gradient was calculated for each point with the two neighbors as 

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:finiteDiff1}
		\left. \frac{dB}{dz} \right|_{z=0} \approx \frac{B_{+0.5} - B_{-0.5}}{1\ \text{mm}}.
\end{equation}

Using a 1-mm separation ($z_2 - z_1$) keeps the approximation in the linear regime while remaining resolvable on the field map. The 1 mm increments in displacement was translated to angular displacement

\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right).
\end{equation}

Furthermore, to determine the magnetic susceptibility $\chi_m$ of the test sample. We use Equations.~(\ref{eq:translationalForce}) and (\ref{eq:Newton_translationalForce}), 

\begin{subequations}
	\begin{equation}
		\chi_m = \frac{\mu_0 m g}{V}\frac{\tan (\alpha)}{ B_0 \frac{dB_0}{dz}},
		\label{eq:chi}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\chi_m}{\rho} = \mu_0 g\frac{\tan (\alpha)}{ B_0 \frac{dB_0}{dz}}.
		\label{eq:chi_rho}
	\end{equation}
	\label{eq:chis}
\end{subequations}

\noindent providing a direct estimation of magnetic susceptibility from angular deflection measurements, provided the field and its gradient are known. A plot of the deflection angle versus the product of field strength and field gradient was created. From it, a linear fit extracted $\chi_m$ from the slope following

\begin{equation}
	\tan (\alpha) =   \frac{\chi_m }{\rho \mu_0 g}  \cdot B_0 \frac{dB_0}{dz}.
	\label{eq:forFit}
\end{equation}

All measurements were logged in Microsoft Excel, and photos/videos were captured via smartphone to document behavior. A minimum of three trials were performed for each current level; however, only the final (most consistent) dataset was retained for analysis. Data were exported to a Jupyter Notebook for clean up and processing with \texttt{Pandas}, then plotted to evaluate $\tan \alpha$ vs. field gradient force. Propagated uncertainties in $\alpha$ and $dB_0/dz$ were computed using standard error propagation formulas. Appendix \ref{sec:appCodeNData} includes sample data from these trials and all analysis code and data are publicly available in \cite{juarezluna2025mrisafety}.

\subsection{In Clinic Measurement - Translational Force}

The translational fixture and its central basket were aligned to scanner isocenter using the scanner lasers, with the zero position marked using tape on the couch surface. The distance from the couch edge to the fixture base was measured to enable reproducible placement of the equilibrium position. Due to the strong magnetic forces near the bore entrance, visible alignment of the basket with the ruler zero was only possible either outside the MR suite or with an empty basket. This meant repeated repositioning of the fixture and multiple attempts to establish a true zero reference, adding considerable time to the protocol.

Measurements were performed using a titanium (Ti) sample as a positive control, given its known paramagnetic response in clinical \gls{mri} contexts. Three replicate trials were conducted with the Ti sample, recording both the distance from isocenter and the observed displacement on the fixture scale at each position. The couch was advanced in 50\,mm increments toward the bore entrance and 100\,mm increments away from it to capture the spatial dependence of the force.

The titanium sample was configured as a loosely coiled spring-like shape, oriented horizontally so that its long axis aligned with the scanner's z-direction (longitudinal field) and its diameter was perpendicular to $B_0$. In contrast, a Medtronic STREAMLINE\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} 6492 unipolar epicardial lead was coiled tightly and oriented vertically (perpendicular to $B_0$), a configuration dictated by its greater thickness and stiffness, which facilitated tighter packing. Recognizing that coil geometry and orientation strongly influence induced force and torque, additional trials were performed using straight segments of both the titanium material and 316L stainless steel. These straight configurations followed the identical incremental positioning protocol.

In total, eight raw data tables were acquired, three for the coiled titanium sample, three for the coiled epicardial lead, one for the straight titanium segment, and one for the straight 316L stainless steel segment. All displacement measurements were documented manually and later digitized for analysis. Also publicly available at \cite{juarezluna2025mrisafety}.

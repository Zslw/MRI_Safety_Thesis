To assess \gls{mri}-induced translational forces, our lab developed a pendulum-style apparatus or fixture following the geometry and physics outlined in ASTM F2052-15 \cite{astmF2052}. This apparatus enables measurement of small deflection angles to derive displacement forces via gravitational torque balance (see Eq.~\ref{eq:deflectionAngle}). The following subsections describe the fixture design and features, the test objects used (including positive controls and epicardial lead holders), the smallest measurable force and associated limitations, and the measurement protocols implemented both in the laboratory (low-field solenoid) and in the clinical 3T MRI environment.

\subsection{Measurement Device and test objects}
\label{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}

Figure \ref{fig:pendulum_full} presents CAD renders of the pendulum fixture (full assembly and detailed views of the upper and lower rings), created in CATIA to document the design intent and engineering drawings (full set in Appendix \ref{app:A1}). Figure \ref{fig:realPendulum} shows corresponding photographs of the constructed apparatus, with real-world implementation of these features.

The fixture was constructed entirely from \gls{pvc} components, including a vertical post of about 1.5 meters and a rigid base of dimensions 30(L)$\times$20(W)$\times$4.5(H) cm for stabilization. The vertical post includes a set of two rings fixed with nylon screws, to adjust heights, a default suspension height of $L= 1$ meter between these two rings was used for consistency across trials, maintaining the geometry and proportions showed in equation (\ref{eq:minForce}). This distances were measured from the last contact point of the string to the rubber on the top ring to the sample hanging free at ruler's height. The lower ring, in Figure \ref{fig:realpendulum_lower}, features a slit where a custom made Kydex\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} ruler is held by smaller nylon screws. This ruler is detachable and allows for easier transportation and ruler size exchange. The upper ring in the CATIA model (Fig. \ref{fig:realpendulum_upper}) was simplified for demonstration, however the final implementation shows a rubber clamp holding the string.

After describing the physical implementation of the pendulum fixture, we now evaluate its measurement capabilities. In particular, we determine the smallest detectable translational  force along the z-axis by analyzing the geometry of the deflected pendulum at equilibrium. Whether forces on the order of millinewtons, comparable to the weight of ~1 g, can be resolved depends on the finite precision of the ruler-based deflection measurement and the pendulum's length.

\subsubsection{Test Objects and Baskets}

%in the lab
Testing for low field lab measurements a solid ferromagnetic cylinder was used as sample, showed in figure \ref{fig:ferrobject}. Its dimensions were captured with caliper, ruler, and scale, yielding length = 6 mm, diameter = 3 mm, and mass = 0.3648 g therefore density $\rho$= 8.6 g/cm$^3$. The sample was suspended by a 955 mm-long sewing thread, tied with a simple knot at its center. For clinical applications, a 0.25 mm nylon monofilament is recommended. A monofilament nylon thread has low stretch,  complies with MR-safe properties (Non-metallic, Non-conductive, Non-magnetic), and reduces variability introduced by torsional drift or thread elasticity \cite{stoianovici2024, astmF2052}. The bottom of the sample was free to swing, forming a pendulum under gravity effectively aligning with model on figure \ref{fig:astm_diagram} and equation \ref{eq:translationalForce}. A horizontal ruler, fixed along the Z-axis, served as the visual reference to quantify lateral displacements.

However, this ferromagnetic test object produces a strong response in the small solenoid field and serves mainly to demonstrate the sensitivity of the pendulum setup in the laboratory. To validate the measurement protocol with materials that are expected to exhibit negligible or zero translational force under clinical \gls{mri} conditions and to characterize the epicardial lead itself, positive controls were prepared from two common biocompatible, non-ferromagnetic metals, titanium and 316L stainless steel wire, both sourced from TEMCo Industrial (USA) and supplied on their standard spools \cite{temco_ti_wire}. 

The straight titanium wire (Surgical Grade 1) had a diameter of 1.02\,mm, a cut length of 2.3\,cm, and a measured mass of 0.1549\,g with handling tape for adhesion. The straight 316L stainless steel wire (annealed medical/industrial grade) had a diameter of 0.76\,mm, a cut length of 2.2\,cm, and a measured mass of 0.1690\,g (including handling tape). An additional titanium coil (mass = 1.7964\,g) was tested as a further positive control to assess geometry effects. 

For the clinical epicardial lead measurements, the lead itself (mass = 0.1386\,g, diameter = 0.46\,mm, length = 378\,mm) was mounted in a custom 3D-printed \gls{pla} basket described below.

%in the clinic
To accommodate epicardial lead positioning while maintaining \gls{mri} compatibility, a lightweight, non-magnetic hexagonal comb mesh was developed and fabricated from \gls{pla}. The mesh dimensions were 16 (L) $\times$ 14 (W) $\times$ 1 (H) mm, for a total volume of less than $100 mm^3$ equivalent to around 100 mg given the 1.24 grams per cubic centimeter ($g/cm^3$) \gls{pla} density. Figure \ref{fig:leadHolder} shows a 3D render of the mesh, with the desired proportions. This mesh was 3D printed and has a final weight of 130mg. The mesh was suspended such that the lead axis was aligned approximately parallel to the magnetic field gradient. The mesh's lightweight design aimed to minimize its contribution to the total magnetic force, however its mass was not explicitly subtracted from the translational force calculations.

\subsection{In Lab Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:experiment1}

The solenoid magnet was particularly useful for generating a variable magnetic field for the translational forces measurement tests. It allows for a range of magnetic fields strengths (B0) at a given distance from the magnet without the need of displacing our fixture. The magnetic field was measured in a different way from Section \ref{sec:labMagnets}. This time it was measured at a stable point along the pendulum trajectory for a given current or magnetic field ($B_0$) and then half a millimeter around that point to find the gradient as per Eq. \ref{eq:force}. The gaussmeter in the \gls{lvi} was operated in a single mode; with rest periods between trials due to the time required to stabilize the pendulum after each adjustment. This methodology is detailed below.

The setup used our custom-made solenoid connected to a Pasco power supply capable to deliver up 8 A acting as variable magnetic field generator for our experiment. The test object, as described in section \ref{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}, was attached to a string pending from the \gls{mri} safe pole and angle of deflection was measured visually with a ruler on the subject horizontal displacement towards the coil. The gaussmeter was positioned to make magnetic field measurements at the same time as angle deflection.

As briefly explained in section \ref{sec:FieldGeneration} the translational stage uses three Thorlabs travel motorized actuators driven via the Kinesis software interface \cite{Thorlabs_Kinesis}. While Kinesis-controlled stages support sub-micrometer incremental motion and repeatability on the order of microns \cite{ThorlabsMotionControl2025}, our experiment only required positional accuracy on the order of 0.5 mm. Thus the stage system offered more than sufficient precision and stability for repeatable setup between trials. The translation speed was set up using Kinesis for 0.3 m/s and the maximum displacement of each actuators was 12 mm. Initially, the test object remains at rest (Figure \ref{fig:translational_off}) once the current is flowing the magnetic field on the solenoid pulls the subject towards it (Figure \ref{fig:translational_on}).

%The in-lab methodology that ultimately worked inverted the logic of the trials. Instead of fixing the current and observing displacement, the displacement itself was fixed. 

We chose increments of 1 mm from the resting position up to 11 mm. Induced currents remained low because the ferromagnetic body barely moved, the translation stage’s full range was used, the subject never entered the solenoid unintentionally, and the subject’s position was always precisely known. For each displacement, the current required to produce a magnetic field strong enough to pull the pendulum to that point was measured and recorded alongside the field flux. A magnetic-field map was then acquired at each corresponding current level, ensuring the equilibrium position and its $\pm$0.5 mm neighbors were carefully sampled. With this method, the value of $B_0$ at equilibrium was known for every current level. The gradient was calculated for each point with the two neighbors. Appendix \ref{sec:appCodeNData} includes sample data from these trials.

The measurements taken for characterizing the local field gradient, at the pendulum's equilibrium position ($B_0$), 0.5 mm below ($B_{-0.5}$), and 0.5 mm above ($B_{+0.5}$) were sampled at least nine times per current level, and gradients were computed using Python pandas library with the central finite difference method (Eq. \ref{eq:finiteDiff}) producing

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:finiteDiff1}
	\frac{dB}{dz} \approx \frac{B_{+0.5} - B_{-0.5}}{1\ \text{mm}},
\end{equation}

Using a 1-mm separation ($z_2 - z_1$) keeps the approximation in the linear regime while remaining resolvable on the field map. The 1mm increments in displacement was translated to angular displacement as

\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Clinic Measurement Protocol}

To guarantee safety and repeatability, the measurement of MRI-induced translational forces on the test items was carried out in accordance with a structured methodology.  Initially, all personal belongings were removed to prevent safety issues. The apparatus, a \gls{pvc} pole equipped with a non-magnetic ruler aligned with the pendulum string, was positioned at a precise distance from the MRI bore. Two sets of measurements were performed sequentially: first using a magnetically susceptible test object, and then with the epicardial lead attached, suspended from a light basket and a heavy basket, respectively, to assess force variations. Moving closer to the bore, a step-wise method was followed to measure displacement at certain translational points. Each measurement phase was completed as per the flowchart-guided process, with detailed data recorded in the a worksheet. The comprehensive protocol, including step-by-step instructions and safety considerations, is provided in Appendix%~\ref{app:} for reference.

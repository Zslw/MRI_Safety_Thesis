\section{Translational Force Measurement via Angular Deflection}
\label{sec:Translational}

The solenoid magnet was particularly useful for generating a variable magnetic field for the translational forces measurement tests. It allows for a range of magnetic fields strenghts (B0) at a given distance from the magnet without the need of displacing our fixture. The magnetic field was measured at a stable point along the pendulum trajectory for a given current or magnetic field ($B_0$) and then half a milimter around that point to find the gradient as per eq. \ref{eq:force}. The gaussmeter in the \gls{lvi} was operated in a single mode; with rest periods between trials due to the time required to stabilize the pendulum after each adjustment. This methodology is detailed below.


\subsection{Measurement Device and Test subjects}
\label{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}

To assess MRI-induced translational forces in a laboratory environment, our lab developed a pendulum-style apparatus following the geometry and physics outlined in ASTM F2052-15 \cite{astmF2052}, showcased in Figure \ref{fig:realPendulum}. The fixture itself predated this project, but no engineering drawings or dimensional records existed. For reproduction and future reconstruction, a representation of it was reconstructed in CAD so that future students or labs can rebuild the geometry without much guesswork. These reconstructed views are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:schematic} with design engineering drawings in appendix \ref{app:A1}. 

The experimental frame was constructed entirely from \gls{pvc} components, including a vertical post of about 1.5 meters and a rigid base of dimensions 30(L)$\times$20(W)$\times$4.5(H) cm for stabilization. The vertical post includes a set of two rings fixed with nylon screws, to adjust heights, a default suspension height of $L= 1$ meter between these two rings was used for consistency across trials, maintaining the geometry and proportions showed in equation (\ref{eq:minForce}). This distances was measured from the last contact point of the string to the rubber on the top ring to the sample hanging free at ruler's height. The lower ring, in Figure \ref{fig:pendulum_lower}, features a slit where a custom made Kydex\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} ruler sits held by smaller nylon screws. This ruler is interchangeable. The upper ring in the CATIA model (Fig. \ref{fig:pendulum_upper}) was simplified for demonstration, however the final implementation shows a rubber clamp from which a string holds.

Testing for low field lab measurements a solid ferromagnetic cylinder was used as sample, showed in figure \ref{fig:ferrobject}. Its dimensions were captured with caliper, ruler, and scale, yielding length = 6 mm, diameter = 3 mm, and mass = 0.3648 g therefore density $\rho$= 8.6 g/cm$^3$. The sample was suspended by a 955 mm-long sewing thread, tied with a simple knot at its center. For clinical applications, a 0.25 mm nylon monofilament is recommended. \subsection{In Lab Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:experiment1}

Magnetic field measurements were performed using the AlphaLab Inc. Gaussmeter described in section \ref{sec:FieldGeneration}. The setup used our custom-made solenoid connected to a Pasco power supply capable to deliver up 8 A acting as variable magnetic field generator for our experiment. The subject, as described in section \ref{sec:TransDevice_Testsubjects}, was attached to a string pending from the \gls{mri} safe pole and angle of deflection was measured visually with a ruler on the subject horizontal displacement towards the coil. The Gaussmeter was positioned such that it allowed for small displacements and magnetic field measurements at the same time as angle deflection.

Initial trials attempted to map the magnetic field from the furthest position of the pendulum, 8 cm from the solenoid face, at a constant solenoid current in a single run. The idea was to obtain ($B_0$) at the pendulum’s resting position and then compute the field gradient at any point from that map. Afterward, the pendulum was allowed to be slowly pulled toward the solenoid to its equilibrium position so its displacement (in cm) could be recorded. This was repeated while increasing the solenoid current in 0.5 A steps, aiming to fit the resulting equilibrium positions using Eq. \ref{eq:forFit}. These attempts produced inconsistent results that pointed to errors inherent to the setup. Because the current, rather than the entire apparatus, was changed, the value of $B_0$ at the pendulum’s resting position did not correspond to the required by Eq. \ref{eq:forFit}. Even using the mapped field, estimating the correct value for the magnetic field for each equilibrium position was unreliable. Moreover, predicting equilibrium positions before running the experiment proved difficult. Additional complications came from solenoid imperfections, current leakage, and induced currents, all of which became significant once displacements exceeded 1 cm. At higher fields the pendulum was sometimes pulled fully into the solenoid. Another facor was the gaussmeter restriction on accurate motion tracking to roughly 11–12 mm on the translation stage. While this approach resembles the proposed clinical protocol, the scale of the apparatus and the nonuniform fields make it impractical. Clinically, a single mapping session would give both 
B and $\nabla$B at any point, leaving the angle measurement as the critical step.

The in-lab methodology that ultimately worked inverted the logic of the trials. Instead of fixing the current and observing displacement, the displacement itself was fixed. We chose increments of 1 mm from the resting position up to 11 mm. This solved several issues simultaneously. Induced currents remained low because the ferromagnetic body barely moved, the translation stage’s full range was used, the subject never entered the solenoid unintentionally, and the subject’s position was always precisely known. For each displacement, the current required to pull the pendulum to that point was measured and recorded. A magnetic-field map was then acquired at each corresponding current level, ensuring the equilibrium position and its $\pm$0.5 mm neighbors were carefully sampled. With this method, the value of $B_0$ at equilibrium was known for every current level. A faster variant of this procedure would measure only at the equilibrium position and within $\pm$1 mm around it. Appendix \ref{sec:appCodeNData} includes sample data from these trials.

The measurements taken for characterizing the local field gradient, at the pendulum's equilibrium position ($B_0$), 0.5 mm below ($B_{-0.5}$), and 0.5 mm above ($B_{+0.5}$) were sampled at least nine times per current level, and gradients were computed using Python pandas library with the central finite difference method (Eq. \ref{eq:finiteDiff}) producing

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:finiteDiff1}
	\frac{dB}{dz} \approx \frac{B_{+0.5} - B_{-0.5}}{1\ \text{mm}},
\end{equation}

The 1mm increments in displacement was translated to angular displacement as

\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)
\end{equation}

Allowing for plotting and interpretation with equation \ref{eq:forFit}.

%\subsection*{Data Collection and Analysis}

All measurements were logged in Microsoft Excel, and photos/videos were captured via smartphone to document behavior. A minimum of three trials were performed for each current level; however, only the final (most consistent) dataset was retained for analysis. Data were exported to a Jupyter Notebook for clean up and processing with pandas. Then plotted to evaluate $\tan \alpha$ vs. field gradient force. Propagated uncertainties in $\alpha$ and $dB_0/dz$ were computed using standard error propagation formulas.


\subsection{In Clinic Measurement Protocol}

To guarantee safety and repeatability, the measurement of MRI-induced translational forces on the test items was carried out in accordance with a structured methodology.  Initially, all personal belongings were removed to prevent safety issues. The apparatus, a \gls{pvc} pole equipped with a non-magnetic ruler aligned with the pendulum string, was positioned at a precise distance from the MRI bore. Two sets of measurements were performed sequentially: first using a magnetically susceptible test object, and then with the epicardial lead attached, suspended from a light basket and a heavy basket, respectively, to assess force variations. Moving closer to the bore, a step-wise method was followed to measure displacement at certain translational points. Each measurement phase was completed as per the flowchart-guided process, with detailed data recorded in the a worksheet. The comprehensive protocol, including step-by-step instructions and safety considerations, is provided in Appendix%~\ref{app:} for reference.

\section{Translational Force Measurement via Angular Deflection}

\subsection{Measurement Device}

To assess MRI-induced translational forces in a laboratory environment, Creighton University developed a pendulum-style apparatus following the geometry and physics outlined in ASTM F2052-15\cite{astmF2052}. The vertical post includes a screw clamp mechanism that enables height adjustment; however, a default suspension height of $L= 1$ meter was used for consistency across trials.

The experimental frame was constructed entirely from \gls{pvc} components, including a vertical post of about 1.5 meters and a rigid base for stabilization. Attached to the pole a set of rings were attached to the pole with a nylon screw to held positions. The lower one features a slit, where a custom made Kydex ruler sits held by smaller nylon screws. This ruler is interchangeable. The upper ring shows a rubber clamp from which a string passes. Then the length of suspension, $L= 1$ meter, was set as the distance between these two rings, specifically from the last contact point of the string to the rubber on the top ring to the sample hanging free at ruler's height as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}.

The sample was a solid ferromagnetic cylinder of length = 6 mm, diameter = 3 mm, and mass = 0.3648 g therefore density $\rho$= 8.6 g/cm$^3$). It was suspended by a 955 mm-long sewing thread, tied with a simple knot at its center. For clinical applications, a 0.25 mm nylon monofilament is recommended. A monofilament nylon thread has low stretch,  complies with MR-safe properties (Non-metallic, Non-conductive, Non-magnetic), and reduces variability introduced by torsional drift or thread elasticity \cite{stoianovici2024, astmF2052}. The bottom of the sample was free to swing, forming a pendulum under gravity. A horizontal ruler, fixed along the Z-axis, served as the visual reference to quantify lateral displacements.

\subsubsection*{Lead Holder Frame Design}

To accommodate epicardial lead positioning while maintaining MRI compatibility, we propose the use of a lightweight, non-magnetic bee comb mesh fabricated from \gls{pla}. The suggested mesh dimensions are 16 mm length by 14 mm wide, with a thickness of 1 mm, for a total volume of less than $100 mm^3$ equivalent to around a tenth of 1 gram given the 1.24 grams per cubic centimeter ($g/cm^3$) \gls{pla} density. As shown in figure \ref{fig:leadHolder}.

The mesh needs to be suspended such that the lead axis was aligned approximately parallel to the magnetic field gradient (Z-axis). The mesh's lightweight design aimed to minimize its contribution to the total magnetic force, however its mass was not explicitly subtracted from the translational force calculations.

\subsubsection*{In Lab Measurement Protocol}

Magnetic field measurements were performed using the \textbf{AlphaLab Inc. Gaussmeter} described before. The setup consisted of a custom-made solenoid connected to a Pasco power supply capable to deliver up 8 A acting as variable magnetic field generator for our experiment, the subject - a small ferromagnetic rod - was attached to a string pending from the \gls{mri} safe pole and angle of deflection was measured visually with a ruler on the subject horizontal displacement towards the coil. The Gaussmeter was positioned such that it allowed for small displacements and magnetic field measurements at the same time as angle deflection.

To characterize local field gradients, measurements were taken at three positions: at the pendulum's equilibrium position, 0.5 mm below, and 0.5 mm above, yielding values of $B_{-0.5}$, $B_0$, and $B_{+0.5}$, respectively. Each position was sampled nine times per current level, and gradients were computed using python pandas library and the central finite difference method:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dB}{dz} \approx \frac{B_{+0.5} - B_{-0.5}}{1\ \text{mm}}
\end{equation}

Once the pendulum reached equilibrium under each magnetic condition, the horizontal displacement on $z$ was recorded visually using the ruler as a reference. With known suspension length $L$, the deflection angle $\alpha$ was calculated as:

\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)
\end{equation}

In the clinical MRI environment, $B_0$ may reach 3 T, but the $\chi$ of implanted materials is often orders of magnitude smaller. This inverse balance preserves the translational force regime, and supports translating results from lab simulations to clinical conditions.

\subsubsection{In Clinic Measurement Protocol}

To guarantee safety and repeatability, the measurement of MRI-induced translational forces on the test items was carried out in accordance with a structured methodology.  Initially, all personal belongings were removed to prevent safety issues. The apparatus, a \gls{pvc} pole equipped with a non-magnetic ruler aligned with the pendulum string, was positioned at a precise distance from the MRI bore. Two sets of measurements were performed sequentially: first using a magnetically susceptible test object, and then with the epicardial lead attached, suspended from a light basket and a heavy basket - respectively- to assess force variations. Moving closer to the bore, a step-wise method was followed to measure displacement at certain translational points. Each measurement phase was completed as per the flowchart-guided process, with detailed data recorded in the MEASUREMENT WORKSHEET. The comprehensive protocol, including step-by-step instructions and safety considerations, is provided in Appendix%~\ref{app:} for reference.

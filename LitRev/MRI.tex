% 1. Introduction

\gls{mri} or Magnetic Resonance Imaging is a medical imaging modality that uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency (\gls{rf}) energy to produce images, rather than ionizing radiation like X-rays making it inherent safe. Moreover, \gls{mri} machines are capable of archiving high contrast sensitivity to soft tissue differences because of the reliance on micromagnetic properties of the tissue and its inherent differences leveraging on \gls{pd} and the relaxation phenomena.

% 2. Magnetic Interactions

\gls{mri}, as the name suggests, leverages on the magnetic properties of matter to capture information ultimately forming an image. It is important, then, to understand these properties and how they are characterized. In a particle scale these effects are shown in protons and neutrons within the nucleus due to their nuclear spin and charge distribution. Hydrogen (specifically its proton, $^1H$ ) is the most utilized element in clinical MRI because it has the largest magnetic moment and is highly abundant in biological tissues, primarily in water and fat. 

When placed in a strong static magnetic field ($B_0$), these protons align in parallel (low-energy state) and antiparallel (high-energy state) directions. A slight majority align in the low-energy, parallel direction, resulting in an observable net magnetic moment for the sample.  As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:proton_alignment}, the net magnetic moment of hydrogen protons forms the basis of MRI signal generation.

\subsubsection{The Static Field}
% 1. - Main Magnet                                   [🔴 High Priority]
%      (Primary source of static magnetic field and spatial gradients.)

Magnets are at the core of MR systems, and their performance is judged by field strength, temporal stability, and homogeneity. The strength of a magnetic field, also known as magnetic flux density ($B$), is measured in Tesla (T). An older, alternative unit is the gauss (G), where 1 T = 10,000 G. For perspective, Earth's magnetic field is significantly weaker, around 0.00005 T (0.05 mT or 0.5-1.0 G).

Magnetic fields can be induced by moving charges in a wire, where the direction depends on the charge's sign and direction, described by the "right-hand rule". Coiling a current-carrying wire, creating a solenoid or electromagnet, superimposes these magnetic fields, significantly augmenting the field strength inside the coil and causing a rapid falloff outside. The amplitude of the current and the number of coil turns determine the overall magnitude of the magnetic field strength as follows:

% Magnetic field of a solenoid
\begin{equation}
	B = \mu_{0} \, n I
	\label{eq:solenoid}
\end{equation}

Where B is the magnetic field, $\mu_{0}$ is the vacuum permeability, $n$ is the number of turns and $I$ is the current. Common \gls{mri} are typically made of superconducting wire-wrapped cylinders. These wires are made from niobium-titanium alloys and show no resistance to electric current when maintained at extremely low temperatures. The wire-wrapped cylinder produce a magnetic field parallel to the cylinder's long axis, usually horizontal (along the patient's cranial-caudal axis in clinical systems).

Let us then follow the physics of a static magnetic field and is interaction with matter with electromagnetism theory, any material placed in a magnetic field develops a magnetization, $\mathbf{M}$, defined as the net magnetic dipole moment per unit volume:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{M} \equiv \frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{V}
	\label{eq:magnetization}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the sum of the microscopic magnetic dipoles within the volume $V$ \cite{griffiths2013electrodynamics}. In practice, the microscopic magnetic field inside a material fluctuates rapidly due to atomic and electronic motion. To describe macroscopic behavior, we use an average field over many atoms, and $\mathbf{M}$ represents this ``smoothed'' magnetization.

Different materials respond differently to an applied field. Diamagnetic materials, such as water, calcium, and most organic compounds, develop a magnetization opposite to the applied B-field due to paired electrons. Paramagnetic substances, like molecular oxygen, deoxyhemoglobin, or gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents, align weakly with the field, enhancing the local magnetic flux. The degree to which a material becomes magnetized is quantified by its magnetic susceptibility, $\chi$, which is typically small in biological tissues ($|\Delta \chi| < 0.01$). Ferromagnetic materials, by contrast, exhibit strong self-sustained magnetization but are generally avoided in MRI due to safety hazards.

To simplify the analysis of materials in magnetic fields, physicists define the auxiliary magnetic field $\mathbf{H}$:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{H} \equiv \frac{1}{\mu_0} \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{M}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{B}$ is the macroscopic magnetic flux density and $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space. $\mathbf{H}$ accounts for the part of the magnetic field produced by free currents, separating it from the bound currents induced by magnetization. This distinction becomes useful when analyzing mechanical effects on magnetic dipoles.

Magnetic dipoles interact mechanically with a magnetic field through forces and torques. A dipole $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ in an inhomogeneous field experiences a force:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{F} = (\boldsymbol{\mu} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{B}
	\label{eq:force}
\end{equation}

\noindent and a torque:

\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{\mu} \times \mathbf{B}
	\label{eq:torque}
\end{equation}

In general, forces are described as a function of the static field strength ($B_0$), its spatial gradient ($\nabla B_0$), and the magnetic susceptibility ($\chi$) and density ($\rho$) of the material. A well known and expected behavior is the magnetic pull on high susceptibility materials toward regions of higher field strength, like near the bore entrance of the scanner where the spatial gradient is highest \cite{aboyewa2021,bushberg2011,panych2018}. For instance, a nonmagnetic stainless steel wire with $\chi \approx 103$ ppm may experience a force equivalent to $\sim$30\% of its weight, whereas ferromagnetic materials like pure nickel can experience forces exceeding 20,000 times their own weight. That is more than enough to become dangerous projectiles unless restrained \cite{aboyewa2021,panych2018}.

ASTM F2052-21 offers a standardized technique for assessing magnetically induced displacement force on medical equipment in order to set safety standards for such situations. This standard states that a device is MR Conditional if the deflection force it encounters in the MRI environment is less than the force of gravity upon it. This is usually verified if the deflection angle from vertical is less than 45$^\circ$ \cite{stoianovici2024,astmF2052}.

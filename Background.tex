\section{Concluding Remarks}

This chapter has reviewed the essential theoretical foundations for evaluating MRI safety in patients with retained epicardial leads. MRI fundamentals, static field ($B_0$), RF excitation, gradient encoding, and relaxation ($T_1$, $T_2$), were explained to provide enough information about the scanner function. To assess the interaction with implants, the emphasis was on the static field. It is capable of inducing translational forces and torques on conductive implants, and the physics that rule the interaction seeking to characterize such forces. Magnetic susceptibility ($\chi_m$) was used to classify materials as diamagnetic, paramagnetic, or ferromagnetic, with most human tissues and the primary constituents of modern epicardial leads (MP-35N conductors, platinum-iridium or titanium electrodes, and polymer insulation) occupying the low-to-moderate paramagnetic range.

Epicardial leads, as parts of CIEDs, differ from transvenous leads in placement, design (unipolar vs.\ bipolar, coaxial vs.\ coradial), fixation (suturing vs.\ screwing), and material composition (paramagnetic electrodes and conductors; diamagnetic insulation). Their direct attachment to the epicardium and frequent abandonment (often as 5--30\,cm fragments, 0.5--1.5\,mm diameter) raise concern for mechanical interactions with \gls{mri} scanner fields, despite low-to-moderate magnetic susceptibilities that suggest minimal risk \cite{Schenck2000}.

Safety hazards associated with the three primary field components were systematically examined. Gradient fields produce acoustic noise and possible peripheral nerve stimulation, radiofrequency pulses can induce localized heating via the antenna effect, and the static field generates both translational forces, maximized at bore entrances where spatial gradients are steepest, and torques on conductive implants \cite{Schenck2000,panych2018}.

Current guidelines (HRS 2017, ESC 2021, CMS) remain conservative, considering systems with abandoned or epicardial leads non--MRI-conditional due to insufficient data, despite evidence that post-2000 leads may be safe under monitoring \cite{vuorinen2021,schaller2021}. 

The material presented here serves as a physical framework needed to move from theoretical prediction to empirical validation. Quantifying static-field forces and torques on epicardial lead under controlled conditions in the next experimental chapter addresses the evidence gap that currently restricts \gls{mri} access for a growing patient population.

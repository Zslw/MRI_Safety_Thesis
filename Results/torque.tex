In parallel with Section \ref{sec:metsTorque}, this section is divided into laboratory and clinical work. This experiment has its bases on applying a magnetic field to a sample that is free to rotate on a low friction surface and align with the magnetic field $B_0$. The sample experiences a torque given by Equation \ref{eq:maxtorque} and the counter-force applied by the level arm is measured in our sensor as voltage change.

\subsection{Torque Measurements in the Laboratory Setting}
\label{sec:restorqueLab}

The aluminum-based sensor was calibrated prior to any experiment following methodology described in Section \ref{sec:sensorCal}. Figure \ref{fig:bestRod} shows the resulting calibration curve, from which the slope was obtained in units of V/N.

\begin{figure}[htb!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Assets/bestRodfit.png}
	\caption[Calibration results for low-torque aluminum rod]{Calibration of aluminum sensor for masses ranging from 0 to 200 mg.}
	\label{fig:bestRod}
\end{figure}

The smallest measurable force the instrument can be determined from the inverse of the slope $k = 1/m = 1/3.64 = 0.275$ N/V. Given a minimum measurable voltage was $\Delta V = 0.000885$, the smallest measurable weight was $W_{min} = 0.243\pm 0.003 \text{mN}$,corresponding to minimum measurable torque $\tau_{min} = 0.012 \pm 0.002$ mN $\cdot$m for the 5 cm transfer arm.

As described in Section \ref{sec:InLab_Torque}, torque measurements were performed under two different experimental configurations. First, an anti-Helmholtz coil configuration that increases magnetic field strength at its center with varying current. Second, a set of neodymium magnets which field strength is controlled by distance and number of magnets. Both measured with gaussmeter.

When a very small uniform magnetic field ranging from 0.335 to 3.85 mT was applied using Helmholtz coils, the torque produced by the short (2 cm) ferromagnetic test cylinder remained at or below the detection limit of the measurement system. Only the longer rod yielded reliable results under a similar magnetic field range (0.36 to 2.825 mT). Figure \ref{fig:torque_longerRod} shows the maximum torque measured against the magnetic flux, exhibiting a quadratic dependence on $  B  $. The data was also fit to a second order polynomial, however the extra fitting parameters did not significantly improved the fit. The dominant quadratic term ($  k B^2  $) is in agreement with the theoretical expectation for the torque on a magnetic dipole in a uniform field, where $  \tau \propto B^2  $.

\begin{figure}[htb!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Assets/fig_torque_LR.png}
	\caption[Torque vs. magnetic field strength for the longer cylinder under Helmholtz coils]{Maximum measured torque in mN$\cdot$m as a function of applied magnetic flux density in mB for the longer cylinder under a Helmholtz coil configuration. On the left, a zero intercept fit. Right, a full quadratic fit.}
	\label{fig:torque_longerRod}
\end{figure}

Because of the difficulty quantifying torque for the short cylinder (2 cm) under the low magnetic field from the Helmholtz coil, a second set of measurements was performed using significantly stronger magnetic fields generated by four neodymium magnets. Figure \ref{fig:torque_shorterRod} shows a polynomial relationship. In that case, notice that the 
measured values required approximately an order of magnitude larger torque to reach higher magnetic fields (roughly 1.5–2×) compared to those in Figure \ref{fig:torque_longerRod}.

The zero-intercept fit yielded $k = 7 \pm 0.2 (3.4\%) \text{N}\cdot\text{m/T}^2$, consistent with the quadratic dependence observed for the long rod. The three-parameter fit returned a comparable $k =  18 \pm 3.27 (18.7\%) \text{N}\cdot\text{m/T}^2$ but with a linear coefficient b and offset $\tau_0$ both carrying uncertainties above 29\%, indicating these terms are not statistically significant. The zero-intercept model is therefore sufficient to describe both datasets.

Both configurations confirm that the sensor resolves the expected $\tau \propto B^2$ dependence, validating the measurement system prior to the clinical session, whose results are described in the next section.

\subsection{Torque observed in a 3T Siemens Magnetom\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} MRI scanner}

In the clinic, the torque fixture was positioned on the scanner couch and samples were advanced toward the bore entrance following the same couch travel path described in Section~\ref{sec:clinicTorque}. The fixture pole was aligned to maximize the expected torque response, with samples free to rotate against the sensor arm. Both the circuit and the \gls{mri}-safe torque measuring fixture were verified to be functioning properly and circuit calibration was appropriate for clinical measurements. Based on the magnetic field map acquired in the same session (Section~\ref{sec:resClinic_map}), the maximum field experienced by the samples during the closest positioning was approximately $B \approx 1.5$ T at $\sim$0.2 m from the bore entrance.

Prior to mounting samples on the fixture, a qualitative assessment was performed by manually bringing cylindrical samples of titanium and stainless steel near the bore entrance. A slight but perceptible attraction was felt on the stainless steel sample, consistent with its ferromagnetic response observed before the session. The titanium sample produced no such response.

Neither sample produced a sensor-measurable torque deflection when mounted on the fixture at the maximum measured field strength position. The signal remained within the noise baseline throughout. The stainless steel sample used in the fixture was the same small piece from the translational experiment. Absence of a signal might be related to the sample dimensions falling below the effective detection threshold at the available field strength given that torque is related to volume by Equation \ref{eq:maxtorque}. Possible explanations and implications for sample selection are addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:disCon}.
